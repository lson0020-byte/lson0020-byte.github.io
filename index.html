DOOMSDAY REINTERPRETATION
The Doomsday Clock is one of the most recognisable scientific symbols in modern history. It was first created in 1947 by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, a group of researchers who had worked on the Manhattan Project. Following the end of World War II, these scientists were deeply concerned about the catastrophic potential of nuclear weapons. They wanted a way to warn the public about how close humanity was to destroying the world.

The artist Martyl Langsdorf designed the original visual. She chose a clock because it is a universal tool that everyone understands. In this design, “Midnight” does not represent a new day, but rather the end of human civilization. The closer the minute hand moves to midnight, the more dangerous the global situation becomes. In its first year, the clock was set at seven minutes to midnight, a decision based on the growing tension between global powers at the start of the Cold War.
The Doomsday Clock did more than just measure risk; it produced a specific way of seeing the Earth. It framed the entire planet as a single, fragile object that could be lost forever. This perspective suggested that the Earth was a shared responsibility, but one that was primarily controlled by political and military leaders.

[ 2026: The Systemic ]

Today, the state of the planet has become far more complex. As of January 2026, the clock stands at 85 seconds to midnight—the closest it has ever been in history. This current moment is no longer defined by a single bomb, but by what is known as a “Poly-crisis”. We now live in a world where technical infrastructures, such as AI algorithms and synthetic biology, are deeply embedded within the Earth’s natural cycles.

The modern planet is published through sensors, networks, and constant measurement. We are facing “Non-linear Acceleration,” where risks like Algorithm Decay (the erosion of truth by AI) and Biosphere Tipping Points (ecological collapse) are happening simultaneously. In this post-digital age, the threat is no longer a distant explosion; it is a silent, systemic decay of the very networks that keep our world functioning.

The Need For A Multi-Dimensional Scale

To monitor the planet in 2026, we can no longer look at one single clock hand. We need a new way to categorise the fragments of our global risks.

1. Nuclear Asymmetry

Modern nuclear threats are no longer just about two superpowers. We now face “asymmetric” risks from regional conflicts and smaller, tactical weapons. A single clock hand cannot monitor multiple, different nuclear flashpoints happening at the same time.

2. Algorithm Decay

Generative AI and automated systems are destroying our shared sense of truth. When “reality” is corrupted by algorithms, society loses its ability to solve problems. The Clock measures human decisions, but AI risks are “non-human” and move much faster than any physical pointer.
