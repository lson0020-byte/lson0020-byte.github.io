[ 0.1 The Doomsday Clock ]

The Original Warning System

The Doomsday Clock is one of the most recognisable scientific symbols in modern history. It was first created in 1947 by the Bulletin of the Atomic Scientists, a group of researchers who had worked on the Manhattan Project. Following the end of World War II, these scientists were deeply concerned about the catastrophic potential of nuclear weapons. They wanted a way to warn the public about how close humanity was to destroying the world.

The artist Martyl Langsdorf designed the original visual. She chose a clock because it is a universal tool that everyone understands. In this design, “Midnight” does not represent a new day, but rather the end of human civilization. The closer the minute hand moves to midnight, the more dangerous the global situation becomes. In its first year, the clock was set at seven minutes to midnight, a decision based on the growing tension between global powers at the start of the Cold War.

The Doomsday Clock did more than just measure risk; it produced a specific way of seeing the Earth. It framed the entire planet as a single, fragile object that could be lost forever. This perspective suggested that the Earth was a shared responsibility, but one that was primarily controlled by political and military leaders.

[ 0.2 The Planet in 2026 ]

New Risks, New Problems

Today, the state of the planet has become far more complex. As of January 2026, the clock stands at 85 seconds to midnight—the closest it has ever been in history. This current moment is no longer defined by a single bomb, but by what is known as a “Poly-crisis”. We now live in a world where technical infrastructures, such as AI algorithms and synthetic biology, are deeply embedded within the Earth’s natural cycles.

The modern planet is published through sensors, networks, and constant measurement. We are facing “Non-linear Acceleration,” where risks like Algorithm Decay (the erosion of truth by AI) and Biosphere Tipping Points (ecological collapse) are happening simultaneously. In this post-digital age, the threat is no longer a distant explosion; it is a silent, systemic decay of the very networks that keep our world functioning.

Earth is increasingly understood as a monitored system of data, networks, and planetary signals.

[ 0.3 The New Clock System ]

A Multi-Risk Planet

To monitor the planet in 2026, we can no longer look at one single clock hand. We need a new way to categorise the fragments of our global risks.

1. Nuclear Weapons

Modern nuclear threats are no longer just about two superpowers. We now face “asymmetric” risks from regional conflicts and smaller, tactical weapons. A single clock hand cannot monitor multiple, different nuclear flashpoints happening at the same time.

2. Digital Threats

Generative AI and automated systems are destroying our shared sense of truth. When “reality” is corrupted by algorithms, society loses its ability to solve problems. The Clock measures human decisions, but AI risks are “non-human” and move much faster than any physical pointer.

3. Biological Risks

Advances in synthetic biology mean that dangerous pathogens can now be engineered in labs. The line between a natural pandemic and a man-made disaster is disappearing. A clock represents a single “moment” of impact, but biological threats are invisible, constant, and leak slowly into the environment.

4. Environmental Shift

Ecological collapse is not a straight line; it is a series of “tipping points.” Once a forest or an ocean reaches a certain limit, the damage becomes irreversible. The Doomsday Clock is “human-centered.” It fails to show the independent, complex feedback loops of the natural world.

[ 0.5 Four Risk Categories ]

Nuclear weapons are the most destructive, inhumane and indiscriminate weapons ever created. Both in the scale of the devastation they cause, and in their uniquely persistent, spreading, genetically damaging radioactive fallout, they are unlike any other weapons. A single nuclear bomb detonated over a large city could kill millions of people. The use of tens or hundreds of nuclear bombs would disrupt the global climate, causing widespread famine.

Nuclear weapons are unique in their destructive power and the threat they pose to the environment and human survival. They release vast amounts of energy in the form of blast, heat and radiation. No adequate humanitarian response is possible. In addition to causing tens of millions of immediate deaths, a regional nuclear war involving around 100 Hiroshima-sized weapons would disrupt the global climate and agricultural production so severely that more than a billion people would be at risk of famine.

In 1946, the United States conducted this nuclear test at Bikini Atoll. Tests moved underground in the 1960s to limit nuclear fallout. After decades of hiatus, the United States may resume underground tests, some experts say.

The two nuclear bombs dropped on Japan in 1945 killed and maimed hundreds of thousands of people, and their effects are still being felt today.

Digital Threats
01:10:00 TO MIDNIGHT

When the United Nations Environment Assembly convenes in December, a key topic up for discussion will be the mounting environmental impact of artificial intelligence. Ahead of those talks, here’s a look back at a story first published on 21 September 2024. There are high hopes that artificial intelligence (AI) can help tackle some of the world’s biggest environmental emergencies. Among other things, the technology is already being used to map the destructive dredging of sand and chart emissions of methane, a potent greenhouse gas. But when it comes to the environment, there is a negative side to the explosion of AI and its associated infrastructure, according to a growing body of research. The proliferating data centres that house AI servers produce electronic waste. They are large consumers of water, which is becoming scarce in many places. They rely on critical minerals and rare elements, which are often mined unsustainably. And they use massive amounts of electricity, spurring the emission of planet-warming greenhouse gases.

Biological Risks
02:40:00 TO MIDNIGHT

Risks from biological hazards:
Biological hazards pose risks for many workers. The risk of exposure is not always obvious.

0.1 Animals and animal products

People who work with live animals or animal products, such as blood, tissue, milk and eggs, are exposed to animal diseases and infections. Some of these can infect people, like avian flu or Hendra virus, or cause serious allergy from being sensitised

Factory farms crowd large numbers of animals together in confined, enclosed spaces with high levels of aerial pollutants like ammonia and faecal dust. Image Credit: We Animals Media

0.2 Human bodily matter

Workers in health care may be exposed to biological hazards through contact with human bodily matter, such as blood, tissue, saliva, mucus, urine and faeces. These substances have a high risk of containing viral or bacterial diseases.

Exposure to hepatitis viruses has long been recognized as an occupational risk for health care personnel (HCP).

0.3 Moulds and yeasts

In some industries, exposure to moulds and yeasts is common. This is the case in workplaces with air conditioning systems and high humidity, and in the construction industry.

Yeasts and molds are ubiquitous fungi that frequently contaminate and spoil commercially-produced foods, resulting in reduced shelf life and potential health hazards

0.4 Organic material

In the work environment, people may be exposed to rubbish, waste water and sewerage, plant materials, organic dusts and food.

It’s believed that around 9-12 million tons of plastic reach the ocean every year, which is a number that needs to be reduced substantially to make sure that the quality of ocean water doesn’t worsen even more.

[ 06. After Midnight ]

Living With Planetary Risk

85 SECONDS TO MIDNIGHT

The original Doomsday Clock reduced global danger into a single countdown toward catastrophe.

This reissue expands the Clock into a distributed planetary system. Instead of one universal timeline, it visualises multiple forms of risk — nuclear instability, algorithmic decay, biological engineering, and ecological collapse — each moving toward midnight at different speeds.

The project reframes the Clock not as a singular warning symbol, but as a continuous interface for monitoring interconnected systems of planetary risk.

The question is no longer how close we are to midnight,

but how we continue living within systems already approaching collapse.

While the Doomsday Clock measures macro-systemic crises on a global scale, this final section brings the Risk Scale down to the texture of daily life. By overlaying the countdown metrics onto personal photographs of ordinary moments—a seagull on the shore, night traffic, or an evening commute—this visual diary captures the psychological reality of our era: how human routines, habits, and small moments of peace quietly persist under the shadow of a fracturing world.
